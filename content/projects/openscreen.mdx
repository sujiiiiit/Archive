I learn things by taking them apart. Most of what I know came from opening something I liked and rebuilding it until I understood why it felt the way it did.

The screen recorders that make demos look good are a lot of things at once: a native capture engine, a video editor, a little physics engine for the camera, and a renderer that has to export exactly what you saw in the preview. I wanted to know how all of that fits together, so on 11 May I made a new folder.

## Getting pixels off the screen

The first week was all native code. Electron can capture the screen on its own, but not the way I wanted, so the recorder is an Objective-C++ addon that talks to ScreenCaptureKit, AVFoundation and VideoToolbox directly. By day three, that addon was one file of 2,548 lines. I split it into thirteen.

<Commit>split monolithic addon.mm into focused modules</Commit>

Then came the part I didn't expect: making a recording look like the screen. My first version captured in 10-bit Display-P3, the way OBS does it, and I was proud of it for about a week. Then I put a recording next to a plain screenshot and the recording looked washed out. The fix was to let ScreenCaptureKit convert to sRGB on the GPU before the frames ever reached me.

Text was soft too, even at the right resolution. Video encoders are tuned for camera footage, and screens aren't camera footage. Three settings fixed it:

- **No B-frames.** They borrow detail from future frames, and on text that shows up as a faint smudge.
- **A keyframe every second**, so fine detail doesn't drift on a screen that barely moves.
- **A bitrate four times the old default.** The old one was the main reason everything looked soft.

## The things nobody sees

A recorder is mostly edge cases. A few I'm fond of:

- The window picker kept getting stuck on the macOS desktop where it first opened. Hiding and showing it again re-anchored it there, so it stopped hiding.
- Pause doesn't leave a gap. Every sample after a pause is shifted back by however long you paused, so the file plays straight through.
- Quitting mid-recording used to leave a broken file, because the video's index is only written when the writer finishes. Now quitting waits for that.
- Starting a recording took half a second to a second, just asking macOS what's on screen. Now the app asks early, while you're still picking a window.

## An editor, and then a different editor

On 17 May the recorder became an app with an editor. I built the canvas on Konva first: backgrounds, padding, rounded corners that use the same smooth-corner maths as Figma, a timeline, autosave, undo, and export through WebCodecs and Mediabunny. It all worked.

On 29 May, at 2:43 in the morning, I added a small lab page to compare a motion blur in Konva against the same blur in PixiJS. Three minutes later I deleted the Pixi half.

Six days later I moved the entire editor to Pixi.

> performance of pixijs is way more than konvajs.

That's the whole reason, and it's still the honest one. I planned the move as twelve small milestones behind a feature flag (background, video playback, selection, the zoom camera, export) so the old canvas kept working until the new one could replace every piece of it. The commits for the whole migration land between 7:32 and 10:14 that evening. The last one deletes Konva.

## The bug that waited until after init

Pixi renders with WebGPU when it can and falls back to WebGL when it can't. I wrapped startup in a try/catch so a failure would drop to WebGL, and moved on.

On some machines, the browser says it supports WebGPU and startup succeeds. Then the first texture upload throws, because two constants that WebGPU needs were never defined. By then startup is long over, so my try/catch never saw it. The preview just died mid-frame.

The fix is a few lines: check for those constants before choosing WebGPU at all. Finding it took a lot longer than writing it.

<Commit>dynamic WebGPU/WebGL preference handling</Commit>

## The cursor

A recorded cursor shakes, because hands shake. The usual fix is a spring that follows the real position, but a spring always runs a little behind you.

I didn't need that trade-off. The cursor is recorded separately from the video, so by the time you edit, I already know where it goes next. I smooth the path forwards, then smooth it again backwards. The two delays cancel out, so the shake is gone and the cursor doesn't lag at all.

```ts
// forwards: each point leans towards the one before it
forward[i] = forward[i - 1] + alpha * (current[i] - forward[i - 1])

// backwards: each point leans towards the one after it
backward[i] = backward[i + 1] + alpha * (forward[i] - backward[i + 1])
```

Because the smoothed path is worked out ahead of time, the cursor's position is a pure function of time. Playing, scrubbing backwards and exporting frame by frame all draw it in exactly the same place.

Later I noticed the cursor flickering from an arrow to a text beam and back whenever it brushed past a text field. Now any cursor shape that shows for less than 150 ms keeps the previous shape.

## The hard part: zooms that know where to look

The hardest part was analysing the cursor data, turning it into zooms on the timeline automatically, and showing them in the preview.

I went through three versions of "where should the camera go". The first anchored on clicks. The second tried to work out which region of the screen you were paying attention to. The third combined the two and tried to stay steady while doing it. Each was cleverer than the last, and each one guessed wrong in new ways.

In the end I threw all three away for something much simpler. Clicks that happen close together become one zoom, and in auto mode the camera follows your cursor on a spring, so it eases instead of snapping. Every zoom lands on the timeline as an ordinary clip, so when it guesses wrong you can drag it, trim it or delete it.

One bug from this part still makes me laugh. Auto zooms worked perfectly, unless you refreshed the page. After a refresh they froze in place and acted like manual zooms. The camera's path was being calculated before the cursor data had loaded, so it was baked from nothing. Changing the order of two updates fixed it.

## Where it stopped

The last commit is from 17 June. Work got busy and the app went on pause. It isn't abandoned, and it isn't public yet.

It records, it smooths the cursor, it zooms on its own, and what you export matches what you saw in the preview. I understand how the good ones work now, which was the point.
